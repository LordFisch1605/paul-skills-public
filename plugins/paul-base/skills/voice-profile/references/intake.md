# Gathering material for a voice profile

## Ask for this, in this order

1. **Messages to a real person** — chat, email, forum posts. Highest yield: written to be
   understood, not to look good. Thinking habits are undisguised here.
2. **Requests to someone with authority** — a manager, an office, a professor. Shows how they
   sequence context and how they ask, which is exactly what applications and cover letters need.
3. **Anything already written in the target register**, even a bad draft. Rare, and worth more
   than everything above.
4. **Drafts they have revised** — the best evidence there is. See the correction-pair loop in
   `SKILL.md` §4.

Roughly 1,000 words minimum, from at least two occasions. Below that, and traits cannot be told
apart from accidents of one day's mood.

## What each type supports

| Material | Supports | Does **not** support |
|---|---|---|
| Chat / messages | thinking habits, hedge frequency, sentence length, recurring errors | the target register, paragraph architecture, how they revise |
| Requests to authority | context-before-ask, politeness strategy, framing | anything long-form |
| Prior work in the target register | structure, register, vocabulary | little — this is the good stuff |
| Revised drafts | everything, and it outranks the rest | nothing |

Write the honest version of this into the profile's data-basis paragraph. A profile that
overstates its evidence is the failure mode; one that understates it just gets extended.

## Reading the material

Read it once without taking notes. The distinctive habit is usually the one that is noticeable
the first time and invisible by the third.

Then count. Concessive connectors, hedges, numerals, sentence-initial subjects, dashes, exclamation
marks. Counting turns "seems to hedge a lot" into "eleven hedges in 1,100 words, of which four
are *allerdings*" — which is checkable, and which makes the next draft measurable against it.

Then separate: is this a **habit of thought** (transfers to any register) or a **surface feature**
(does not)? Getting this wrong in either direction is the main way profiles go bad — dropping a
thinking habit strips the voice out, keeping a surface feature produces a costume.

## Quoting

- Verbatim, typos and all. The typos show the raw tone, and a tidied quote is no longer evidence.
- Indented block quotes, so quotes are visually separable from the method's own prose at a glance.
- Two quotes per trait is plenty; a third is padding.
- Prefer quotes where the trait appears *incidentally*, not where the person is performing.

## Before quoting, cut

- Anything about a third party's private life — health, relationships, conflicts. The profile
  needs the sentence, not the disclosure.
- Anything the person would not want re-read in a work context.
- Names of people who are not the person themselves, where the sentence works without them.

If a quote is the only evidence for a trait and cannot be cleaned, drop the trait and note in the
limits section that it was observed but not recordable.

## When the material is thin

Do not fill the gap with plausible description. Two honest options:

- Build a **short** profile covering only what is evidenced, with a limits section naming what is
  missing and what would close it.
- Ask for more material, naming which kind — see the table above for what each type would add.

A four-section profile that is entirely evidenced is more useful than a twelve-section one that
is half inference, because a drafting session cannot tell the two halves apart.
